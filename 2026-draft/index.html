A welcome from UPAD

Anand Sinha and Sunila Kumar

Saturday, November 14, 2026

Dear Friends and Family,

Aap sabko Deepavali ki haardik shubh kaamnayein!

Diwali reminds us that light grows when we come together—with family, friends, and community. It is a time to celebrate our traditions, renew our bonds, and share the joy of our culture with the next generation.

For more than three decades, UPAD has brought families together through Indian art, culture, literature, and community events. We remain guided by the values that have shaped our association: Hindi-centric, open to all, family-oriented, well-organized, and fun-loving. Our purpose is to strengthen our cultural roots in Michigan while creating a welcoming place for everyone to participate.

UPAD’s future depends on all of us. To our younger families and emerging leaders: please make this community your own. Perform on stage, volunteer, join a committee, lead an initiative, or bring forward a new idea. Your energy and perspective will help UPAD grow while keeping our traditions alive. We warmly invite you to become Life Members and help carry this legacy forward for our children and grandchildren.

With heartfelt appreciation, we thank our members, volunteers, sponsors, advertisers, performers, parents, and guests. Your generosity and seva make this celebration—and UPAD’s work throughout the year—possible.

On behalf of the UPAD Board of Trustees, Executive Committee, and Executive Members, welcome to our 2026 Diwali celebration. May the festival bring light, health, harmony, and happiness to your homes. We hope you enjoy the evening and continue building a vibrant UPAD community with us.

With warmest wishes,

Diwali: The Light of Dharma

Anand Sinha

Diwali: The Light of Dharma Anand Sinha

Diwali is not merely a celebration of a homecoming, nor only the commemoration of a victory. Its essence lies in remembering how that victory was achieved and what values guided it.

Shri Ram is honored as Maryada Purushottam — the ideal human being who lives with balance, restraint, integrity, and compassion. His greatness does not lie in power, miracles, or conquest, but in the dignified discipline with which he conducted every moment of his life.

In a world where strength is often confused with dominance, Ram teaches that true strength is self-mastery. He did not allow anger to dictate his decisions, nor sorrow to break his determination, nor love to overshadow his sense of righteousness. He walked the path of Dharma even when it demanded personal sacrifice, discomfort, and silence in the face of misunderstanding. This is the courage of character that Diwali asks us to reflect on. The darkness we speak of during Diwali is not outside. It is the darkness of ego, greed, arrogance, restlessness, and impulsiveness. Ravana was not merely a figure of history; he is the symbol of the human mind when intelligence lacks humility. The conquest of Lanka represents not the defeat of another, but the victory over oneself. Diwali invites us to recognize that the real battle is internal — a lifelong journey of refining intention, speech, and conduct. When a lamp is lit, its flame stands upright. It does not flicker toward the past or bend toward the future. It glows in the present moment — steady, warm, and unwavering. The lighting of diyas is therefore not a symbolic gesture of festivity; it is a reminder to keep our inner flame pure — to remain honest when tempted, gracious when tested, and compassionate even when unheard.

Ayodhya, the city to which Ram returned, literally means “a place where inner conflict ends.” Diwali is not about a geographical homecoming, but the return to our own inner peace — the quiet clarity that arises when ego dissolves and the heart stands firm in truth.

This Diwali, may we not only celebrate light, but become it. May we learn to speak gently, act consciously, choose wisely, and anchor every emotion in dignity. To follow Shri Ram is not to worship him from a distance, but to live his values from within.

🪔 May the light of Dharma find a place in our thoughts, our decisions, and our way of being. Wishing you a Diwali of clarity, humility, and inner radiance.

UPAD: A Home for Our Roots, Our Pride, and Our Future

We come from Uttar Pradesh — not just a region, but a civilization that has shaped the spiritual, cultural, and moral identity of Bharat for thousands of years. Ayodhya, Kashi, Mathura, Prayagraj, Chitrakoot, Naimisharanya — these sacred places are not distant names. They are the soil of our memory, the rhythm of our festivals, the stories we grew up with, and the values that formed us.

From the compassion of Shri Ram to the playful wisdom of Shri Krishna, from Tulsidas’s devotion to the Braj and Awadhi songs our grandparents sang, from Kathak’s grace to the Hindustani music that shaped India’s classical voice, Uttar Pradesh has always been a cradle of identity. It has given Bharat its language, literature, music, etiquette, philosophy, cuisine, festivals, and the very foundation of its spiritual imagination.

But when we leave our homeland and begin life in America, something quiet happens. We become busy. We become modern. We also become distant. And yet, our hearts still recognize the sound of a bhajan from childhood… the taste of kadhi-chawal from home… the warmth of saying or hearing “Ram Ram”… the fragrance of mitti when it rains in Awadh.

UPAD exists to honor that connection. It is not an organization; it is a home — where we remember who we are, and where our children learn what it means to belong to something ancient, graceful, and dignified. Here, we celebrate not as audience, but as participants and custodians of heritage — through Ramayan paths, Krishna Leela, folk dances, Holi and Diwali, Purvanchal and Bundelkhand songs, Awadhi tehzeeb, and the stories that shaped us. When our children step onto the stage and perform something rooted in our culture, they do not just learn an art — they receive an identity.

This organization grows when each of us says: “I will give not only my presence, but my heart.” There is so much each of us can do — teach children a prayer you learned from your grandmother, choreograph a folk dance, narrate a story from Ramcharitmanas, bring forward a recipe, help organize an event, mentor a student, volunteer an hour, support a cause, or simply show up with warmth.

When we gather, we are not just arranging programs. We are building a living river of heritage that flows forward into the next generation. Our children should not learn about Ram, Krishna, Kabir, Kashi, or Ayodhya from textbooks and internet searches. They should learn from us — in our voices, with our affection, in our shared festivals, in a community that feels like family. This is our chance to reclaim belonging. This is our chance to strengthen each other. This is our chance to ensure our roots travel with us. UPAD is not only where we gather. It is where we remember who we are — and where our children discover who they are meant to be.

Sonia’s Food Corner: Kakori Kabab

Sonia Sinha

I have always admired the delicate flavors of Awadhi cuisine. Kakori Kabab, named for Kakori near Lucknow, is loved for its tender texture and fragrant spices. This home-style version combines finely ground lamb, fried onion, warm spices, and a little raw papaya for a special starter.

Ingredients (makes about 8 small kababs)

Ground lamb or mutton, with some fat1 lb
Raw papaya paste1 tbsp
Ginger-garlic paste1 tbsp
Fried onion, finely ground¼ cup
Cashew paste1 tbsp
Roasted gram flour2 tbsp
Ground black pepper½ tsp
Ground cumin½ tsp
Kashmiri chili powder½ tsp
Ground green cardamom¼ tsp
Mace1 pinch
Nutmeg1 pinch
Saffron strands, soaked in warm milk6 strands + 1 tbsp milk
Kewra water1 tsp
Salt¾ tsp, or to taste
Ghee, for brushing2 tbsp

Directions

Kakori Kabab

  1. Process the ground meat in a food processor until very fine and smooth.
  2. Mix in the papaya, ginger-garlic paste, fried onion, cashew paste, spices, saffron milk, kewra water, and salt. Cover and refrigerate for 1–2 hours.
  3. Fold in the roasted gram flour a little at a time. Shape the mixture around metal skewers with damp hands. If it feels too soft to hold its shape, chill it a little longer.
  4. Brush lightly with ghee. Grill at 425°F (220°C), turning carefully until browned and cooked through. Ground lamb should reach 160°F inside.
  5. Serve hot with mint chutney, sliced onion, lemon, and sheermal or naan.

Mint Chutney

  • Blend 1 cup mint leaves, ½ cup cilantro, 1 green chili, 1 tbsp lemon juice, salt, and a little water.
  • Add water as needed for a smooth, spoonable consistency.

From Sonia’s Kitchen to your table — wishing you a delicious meal!

Our Civic Responsibility as Citizens

Our Civic Responsibility as Citizens By Anand Sinha | Chair of Social Media and Chief Information Officer, Americans4Hindus (A4H)

For many of us, the United States is our home and our “karam bhoomi”—the place where we live, work, raise families, contribute, and help shape the future. Integration means participating fully in the civic life of this country while carrying our heritage with us. Politics is one part of that shared responsibility. Getting involved in politics is not “becoming an activist.” It is exercising our fundamental rights and taking part in the decisions that affect our families and neighbors. We can serve as elected officials, volunteer on campaigns, attend public meetings, help register voters, or simply become informed and vote consistently.

Start close to home Public service begins at every level. City councils decide local budgets, roads, public safety priorities, and many land-use matters. Planning commissions review development proposals and long-range plans; members are commonly appointed, and their authority varies by community. School boards set district policy and budgets. Parks, library, zoning, and other boards and commissions also give residents a direct voice. Attend a meeting, learn the process, volunteer, apply for an appointment, or consider running for office.

How U.S. Congress works Congress makes federal laws, approves spending, and oversees the executive branch. It has two chambers, with different terms and responsibilities: Chamber Seats and representation Term Key responsibilities 6 years; about one-third Confirms many presidential nominees, approves treaties, U.S. Senate 100 senators; 2 from each state elected every 2 years conducts impeachment trials, and considers legislation.

435 voting representatives; seats Representation is divided into districts; the House originates U.S. House 2 years apportioned among states by population revenue bills and brings federal impeachment charges. Each state has at least one House member. The population count from each decennial census determines how the 435 seats are distributed among states, and states redraw congressional districts. Under the 2020 Census apportionment, Michigan has 13 House seats; that allocation remains until the next reapportionment after the 2030 Census.

Public service, time, and compensation The work and compensation differ by office. In 2026, U.S. senators and representatives receive a base salary of $174,000 a year. Michigan legislators receive $71,685 a year plus a $10,800 annual expense allowance. Congressional service is generally a full-time commitment. Michigan’s legislature is considered part-time, although many members devote full-time hours; schedules and outside employment vary. Many local council and school-board roles are part-time or carry a stipend. Novi states that its mayor and council serve as part-time volunteers without benefits. Planning commission and other appointed roles are often volunteer positions, but local rules differ. There is a role for every schedule and experience level: run for office, serve on a board, volunteer for a candidate, help neighbors understand a ballot, or vote in every election for which you are eligible. Local elections can be decided by small margins, and local decisions shape daily life.

A Practical Financial Road Map for Young Families

Dr. Rajeev Singhal

A Practical Financial Road Map for Young Families Options to consider and questions to ask about fit Dr. Rajeev Singhal, BE, MBA, PhD Finance

As families build careers, support a child’s education, and manage daily responsibilities, it is easy to postpone financial planning. Yet protecting ourselves and our families is also important. Whatever the current income, families can consider manageable ways to save, invest, and prepare for setbacks rather than waiting for a less busy time or a higher income. The areas below offer options to consider under each focus; they are not detailed or exhaustive, but are intended to get you thinking. What may fit depends on each family’s circumstances.

Family Security • Emergency reserve and near-term goals. Some families consider a liquid, low-volatility reserve of about 9–12 months of essential expenses; adjust the target for income stability and household needs. Plan separately for housing, vehicle costs, and a child’s education. • Protection and workplace benefits. Review insurance, a will and guardian designation, and whether an HSA, FSA, HRA, or dependent-care FSA fits your health coverage, expected expenses, and employer plan rules.

Child Education • Compare available paths. Options include a 529 plan (Michigan’s MESP investment-savings plan or MET prepaid tuition), a Coverdell ESA, custodial UGMA/UTMA account, or regular savings or taxable investments. Compare tax treatment, fees, control, qualified uses, flexibility, and possible financial-aid effects. Scholarships, grants, and education credits may also be part of the plan.

Growth Plan • Build a sustainable savings habit. Some families automate contributions, review an employer match, and address costly debt while protecting near-term reserves. For longer-term goals, consider spreading investments across asset types and holdings; match risk to the time available and your comfort with losses. Review fees and rebalance periodically.

Retirement Planning • Compare account choices. Workplace plans such as 401(k)s and 403(b)s, traditional IRAs, and Roth IRAs differ in eligibility, limits, access, and tax timing. Review plan terms and current rules, and compare how each option fits your income and expected future taxes. • Plan for later years. As retirement approaches, consider how withdrawals or Roth conversions may affect taxes and Medicare premiums. IRMAA can raise Part B and Part D premiums for some people; Social Security generally uses tax information from two years earlier.

A fuller explanation of these accounts and strategies will be published in the electronic edition soon.
