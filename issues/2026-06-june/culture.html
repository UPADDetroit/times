The Seven Sacred Cities

Where Simply Being Grants Liberation

There are seven cities in India where simply dying within their boundaries is believed to grant moksha, liberation from the cycle of rebirth. Ayodhya is one of them. But unlike Varanasi with its famous burning ghats, or Haridwar with its Kumbh Mela crowds, Ayodhya has always been quieter about its claim to eternity. The city doesn't announce itself. It simply is.

The other six cities that make up the Sapta Puri—the seven sacred cities—each have their signatures. Mathura is where Krishna was born. Haridwar is where the Ganga descends from the Himalayas. Kashi (Varanasi) belongs to Shiva. Kanchipuram in the South has a thousand temples. Ujjain hosts the massive Kumbh Mela and houses the Mahakaleshwar Jyotirlinga. Dwarka is the legendary capital Krishna built by the sea. But Ayodhya? Ayodhya is where Rama walked. And that has always been enough.

Seventy-Six Steps to Devotion

Why Hanuman Garhi Comes First

There is a ritual in Ayodhya that every pilgrim learns quickly: before you visit the Ram Mandir, you climb seventy-six steps to Hanuman Garhi. It's not optional. It's not folklore. It is what people do. The hilltop temple dedicated to Hanuman sits above the city, and local tradition holds that Hanuman still stands guard over Ayodhya, watching over Rama the way he did during the exile in the forest and the war in Lanka.

The seventy-six steps aren't just a climb. They're preparation. By the time you reach the top—breathing hard, legs burning—something has shifted. The noise of the city fades. The urgency that brought you here quiets down. You're no longer rushing to check off a pilgrimage site. You're present. And that presence is the point.

Hanuman represents devotion without ego, service without expectation of reward. He could have been king, could have demanded recognition for everything he did—leaping to Lanka, finding Sita, bringing the Sanjeevani herb that saved Lakshmana's life. Instead, he chose to remain Rama's servant forever. The tradition of visiting Hanuman first teaches a simple lesson: if you're coming to Ayodhya expecting Rama to grant your wishes, you've misunderstood the relationship. Rama isn't a cosmic vending machine. He's the embodiment of dharma. And dharma doesn't transact. It transforms.

The seventy-six steps aren't just a climb. They're preparation—each step a quieting of demand, a recognition that approaching the divine requires becoming worthy of approach.

The Avatar Circuit

Six Cities Connected by Divine Descent

Ayodhya sits at the center of what you might call the Avatar Circuit—a network of North Indian cities connected by the mythology of divine descent. Drive 300 miles west and you reach Mathura, Krishna's birthplace. Go 250 miles east into Bihar and you hit Gaya, where the Buddha sat under the Bodhi tree and achieved enlightenment. Head 450 miles north to the foothills and you arrive at Haridwar, where the Ganga emerges from the mountains—the river sacred to Vishnu's fish avatar, Matsya, who saved humanity from the flood. Travel 600 miles southeast to Odisha's coast and you find Puri, home of the Jagannath Temple honoring Krishna in his final form.

These aren't random holy sites. They're the geography of incarnation—the places where, according to Hindu belief, the divine broke through into human history. Rama in Ayodhya. Krishna in Mathura. Buddha in Gaya. And still to come: Kalki, the tenth and final avatar, prophesied to arrive at the end of the current age riding a white horse with a flaming sword, bringing an end to the cycle of corruption and restarting the wheel of time.

For many pilgrims, especially those devoted to Sita, the journey doesn't end in Ayodhya. They travel onward to Sitamarhi in Bihar, 280 miles east, where Sita was found as an infant by King Janaka while plowing a field. The town carries her name—literally "Sita's Mother's Home"—and for devotees who see Sita not as Rama's wife but as a divine figure in her own right, visiting Sitamarhi completes the arc from her origin to her destiny.

The Rituals That Never Stopped

But Ayodhya's culture isn't just about grand pilgrimages and ancient cities. It lives in the daily rituals that have survived everything—Mughal conquest, British rule, the five-hundred-year wait for the Ram Mandir. Every evening, hundreds gather at the Sarayu River ghats for aarti. Oil lamps are lit. Prayers are chanted. The river, which has flowed through this landscape for millennia, receives the same offerings it received when Rama bathed in it.

During Deepotsav—Diwali in Ayodhya—the entire city lights millions of earthen lamps. The spectacle is visible from space: a constellation of devotion that turns the city into literal light. It's not a tourist event. It's what Ayodhya does, what it has always done, what it will continue doing long after this year's visitors have returned home. The people who live here don't light lamps to impress outsiders. They light them because this is the City of Light, and they are keeping the tradition alive.

Ram Navami, celebrating Rama's birth, draws pilgrims from across India. They come to bathe in the Sarayu at dawn, to visit the exact spot tradition marks as his birthplace, to participate in prayers that have been recited for centuries. The city swells to ten times its normal population. Hotels fill. Streets overflow. And yet the rituals proceed with the same calm they always have. Ayodhya absorbs the crowd the way the Sarayu absorbs the rain—without resistance, without complaint.

Culture here is not something preserved in museums. It's what people do when no one is watching, because it's what their grandparents did, stretching back through time to Rama himself.

What makes Ayodhya's culture distinct is not its grandeur but its persistence. The city waited five hundred years for the Ram Mandir to be built. Five hundred years of legal battles, political struggles, and collective memory passed down from generation to generation. And through all of it, the rituals continued. The prayers were recited. The festivals were celebrated. The Sarayu kept flowing.

Culture here is not something preserved in museums or performed for audiences. It's what people do when no one is watching, because it's what their grandparents did, and their grandparents before them, stretching back through time to Rama himself. You don't come to Ayodhya to see culture. You come to participate in it. And if you climb those seventy-six steps with the right intention, you might discover that what you thought was ancient history is actually still happening—right now, in you.
