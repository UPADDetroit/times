This issue covers one of the most significant questions in Hindu civilization: who Krishna actually was, what he meant in his own time, and how that meaning has traveled — and been distorted — across 5,000 years. Six sections. One city. One life. Read in sequence, they build toward something.

What Was Destroyed

The Record They Left Behind

We do not need to speculate about what happened to Mathura and Vrindavan over the centuries. The destroyers documented it themselves. Mahmud of Ghazni's court historian, Al-Utbi, recorded in the Kitab-i-Yamini how Mahmud reached Mathura in 1018 and found a city of extraordinary temples — describing their size, their gold, their craftsmanship in detail — before ordering them razed. Sikandar Lodi's biographer recorded how the sultan personally supervised the destruction of temples in Mathura in the early 1500s and forbade Hindus from bathing in the Yamuna. Aurangzeb's own farmans — royal orders, preserved in archives — directed the demolition of the Keshav Dev temple at Krishna Janmabhoomi in 1670 and the construction of a mosque on its plinth.

These are not Hindu grievances. These are the destroyers' own records. When a civilization is told to forget what happened to it, the first act of recovery is simply reading what the record says.

The Keshav Dev temple that Aurangzeb demolished had itself been rebuilt multiple times — once by Vikramaditya in the 4th century CE, once by Akbar's general Man Singh in the 16th century. Each time it was destroyed. Each time it was rebuilt. The pattern tells you something about both the persistence of the destroyers and the stubbornness of those who refused to forget.

What survived was not the architecture. What survived was the memory. The ghats, the forest of Vrindavan, the hill of Govardhan, the grove of Nidhivan — these could not be demolished. They were the original sacred geography, older than any building placed upon them. And it was these — the river, the hill, the forest, the land itself — that kept the tradition alive through centuries when the temples above them were repeatedly torn down.

After Independence

Why Restoration Required Political Will

The restoration of Mathura and Vrindavan didn't happen automatically after independence. Hindu temples in India operate under a legal framework inherited from the colonial era — the HR&CE Acts — under which state governments administer temple revenues and appointments. The result: large-scale restoration at sacred sites required not just community devotion, but the specific political will to direct government resources toward them. For most of the post-independence period, that will was not present in the form the project needed.

At Krishna Janmabhoomi in Mathura specifically, the legal question of the contested land adjacent to the mosque built on Aurangzeb's orders moved through courts for decades. The case remains in process. What changed in the last decade was not the pace of the courts — it was the willingness to pursue the restoration project in parallel, visibly, without waiting for every question to be resolved before anything could be built.

That shift — from waiting to building — is what makes the current moment in Mathura and Vrindavan different from any period since independence. The infrastructure arriving now is the product of political decisions that could have been made earlier and weren't. Understanding that context doesn't require assigning blame for the delay. It simply clarifies why what is happening now is notable, and why it required the particular combination of political commitment and administrative capacity that has arrived in this decade.

The appropriate response will be: this looks like Bharat. It always did. We simply forgot how to see it.

The River

Cleaning the Yamuna — and What It Mirrors

The Yamuna is in crisis. Industrial effluent from the Agra-Mathura corridor has turned long stretches of the river black. Untreated sewage enters the water at dozens of points. The river that Vasudeva crossed with infant Krishna on the night of Krishna's birth — the river that parted to let them through — has been, for decades, one of the most polluted stretches of water in India.

The Yamuna Action Plan is now in its third phase, with sewage treatment plants, river-front development along the ghats of Mathura, and industrial discharge controls all underway. The Braj Teertha Vikas Parishad — the authority established specifically for the restoration of the Braj region — has been coordinating land, water, and infrastructure across the 84-kos Braj Mandal, the traditional sacred geography that encompasses Mathura, Vrindavan, Govardhan, Barsana, and a dozen other sites associated with Krishna's life.

The parallel to the Kaliya episode is not forced. In the Bhagavata Purana, Krishna enters the Yamuna to subdue Kaliya, the serpent whose poison had made the water toxic and killed everything around it. The divine goal was not to kill Kaliya but to clean the river — transformation, not destruction. The river became habitable again. The village could return to the water. The episode encodes, among other things, the idea that sacred rivers must be protected and that their pollution is not a minor administrative matter but a civilizational failure.

That the Yamuna cleaning project is now being pursued seriously — not just announced, but funded, phased, and monitored — is one of the more significant developments in the Braj restoration story. It is also one of the least reported.

What Is Being Built

Chandrodaya Mandir and the Skyline of Devotion

The Chandrodaya Mandir — proposed for Vrindavan — is designed to rise over 700 feet when completed. That would make it the tallest temple in the world, and one of the tallest religious structures of any kind anywhere. The structure is not just scale for its own sake. The architectural vision places the temple in the sky as a visible landmark from miles away — so that arriving in Braj, the first thing a pilgrim sees on the horizon is the temple above the city, the way medieval European cities were oriented around their cathedrals.

The Braj Teertha Vikas Parishad has also been working on the 84-kos parikrama — the traditional circumambulatory route around the entire Braj Mandal, about 170 miles in full. Pilgrims who complete this parikrama on foot often take weeks. The BTVP has been restoring the route, adding waystations, improving roads between the 12 major forests (vanas) associated with Krishna's life, and documenting sacred sites along the way that had become inaccessible or forgotten.

The tourism numbers tell part of the story. Mathura district received an estimated 32 million visitors in 2023. By 2025, that figure had climbed past 45 million. Local businesses — flower sellers, prasad shops, dhaba owners, rickshaw drivers — describe the change not primarily in economic terms but in energy. The city feels different when people come with intention.

The political dimension is real and should be named without either dismissing it or reducing everything to it. Modi and Yogi Adityanath have both invested political capital and government resources in the Braj restoration project. That political will matters — projects of this scale do not happen without it. Whether one agrees with their politics entirely or not, the civilizational project they are facilitating here is one that required someone to make the decision to do it. Restoration at this scale requires not just devotion but institutional commitment.

A Different Reflex

Seeing It for What It Is

There is a reflex that runs through the Indian professional class — including, honestly, within the diaspora — that reaches for foreign comparisons to validate local achievement. The ghats of Mathura are described as "like Venice." The temples of Vrindavan are "like the Vatican." The corridors and planned greenways are "like Switzerland." The comparison is always to somewhere in Europe, always framed as elevation.

A civilization that was debating philosophy, building temples, and mapping the cosmos at a time when its colonizers had not yet found their footing was taught, over two centuries, to measure its worth against the lands of its colonizers. That training did not disappear with independence. It lodged itself in the way educated Indians describe beauty, excellence, and development — always with reference to somewhere else.

The appropriate response, when the restoration is complete, will not be that Mathura looks like Switzerland. It will be: this looks like Bharat. It always did. We simply forgot how to see it.

What is being built in Mathura and Vrindavan is not a museum. It is not heritage tourism infrastructure. It is a living civilization recovering its visible form — the form that was systematically dismantled over centuries and that somehow, in the songs and the rituals and the pilgrims who kept walking through every political dispensation, was never entirely lost. The land remembered, even when the buildings were gone. What is being added now are buildings worthy of the land.
